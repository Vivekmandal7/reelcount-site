The problem

One reel turns into fifty.

Screen time tells you that you spent two hours in an app. It doesn’t tell you that you watched a hundred and forty separate videos, because nobody has ever shown you that number. You can’t break a habit you can’t see.

What screen time shows

2 h 10 m

A length of time. Easy to shrug off.

What ReelCount shows

143 reels

One for every video you swiped to. Much harder to ignore.

How it works

Three steps, then it just counts.

  1. 01

    Turn on counting

    Android calls the permission Accessibility. ReelCount uses it to notice when a new reel appears in a short-video app, and for nothing else. The app shows you exactly what it does before you switch it on.

  2. 02

    Scroll like you always do

    A small counter with Rotly sits at the top of the app and goes up by one for every new reel. Drag it out of the way if it’s covering something. Scroll back to a reel you’ve already seen and it isn’t counted again.

  3. 03

    Pick a daily limit

    Rotly stays fresh while you’re under it, and starts to melt when you’re not. A hundred a day is where most people start. Change it whenever you like.

Privacy

The counting never leaves your phone.

Counting a reel inside another app needs Android’s Accessibility permission. It’s a powerful permission, so here is exactly what ReelCount does with it.

Does
  • Notices when a new reel or short appears in the apps listed above, and adds one to your count.
  • Reads just enough on-screen text to tell one clip from the next, and turns it straight into a fingerprint that can’t be turned back into words.
  • Keeps your counts, limit and streak on your phone.
Never
  • Reads your messages, your passwords or anything you type.
  • Keeps any text from a video, or anything about what it shows.
  • Takes screenshots or records your screen.
  • Sends anything off your phone.

Questions

What people ask first.

Why does ReelCount need the Accessibility permission?

Because it’s the only way Android lets one app notice that a new short video has appeared inside another. ReelCount is told about the short-video apps it counts and nothing else on your phone.

The app explains exactly what it does before you switch it on, and you can switch it off any time in Android settings. Counting stops straight away when you do. The full detail is in the privacy policy.

Is the count exact?

It’s close, and when it’s unsure it leaves a reel out rather than inventing one. A reel counts once it has actually settled on screen, so flicking straight past one doesn’t, and scrolling back to a reel you’ve already seen doesn’t count it twice.

Counting depends on how each app lays out its player, and apps change that without warning. If one does, counting in that app can stop until we ship a fix. The app tells you when that happens rather than showing a number that has quietly stopped moving.

Is there an iPhone version?

Not at the moment. iOS doesn’t let one app see what’s on screen in another, which a live count like this depends on. ReelCount is Android only.

What happens when I reach my limit?

Rotly starts to melt, and that’s all, unless you turn on blocking. With blocking on, the reel player is covered for the rest of the day while the rest of the app keeps working. There’s a button for five more minutes, and strict mode removes it.

Does it cost anything?

ReelCount is free to download, and counting, Rotly, streaks, stats and the widget are free. There’s no account to make.
